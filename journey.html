My Journey: Homelessness & Poverty

This page documents my ongoing work on two problems I refuse to accept as permanent — homelessness and poverty. It's a living record: what I'm learning, what I'm doing, what's working, and what isn't.

Why this matters to me

Homelessness and poverty aren't abstractions — they're people. Behind every statistic is someone navigating impossible choices: rent or food, medicine or heat, a job application with no address to put on it. I started this journey because I believe these problems are solvable, and because waiting for someone else to solve them stopped feeling like an option.

I don't claim to have all the answers. What I have is a commitment to keep showing up, keep learning from the people closest to the problem, and keep putting time, skills, and resources toward solutions that actually move the needle.

What I focus on

Housing first

Stable housing is the foundation everything else builds on. I support approaches that get people housed first, then wrap services around them — because it's nearly impossible to fix anything else while sleeping outside.

Economic dignity

Poverty is a lack of money, not a lack of character. I work on ways to widen access to income, skills, and financial tools so people can build stability on their own terms.

Direct help, now

Systems change takes years; hunger happens tonight. Alongside the long-term work, I believe in immediate, no-strings help — meals, supplies, and treating people on the street like neighbors, not scenery.

What I've learned

  • Listen before you build. The people experiencing homelessness and poverty understand the problem better than any report. Solutions designed without them usually fail.
  • Small and consistent beats big and once. A recurring commitment — hours, dollars, attention — compounds in a way one-off gestures never do.
  • Dignity is the whole point. How help is given matters as much as what is given. Nobody wants to be a project.

How you can help

You don't need to run a nonprofit to matter here. A few places to start:

  • Give locally. Find your local shelter, food bank, or housing nonprofit — they know exactly what your community needs tonight.
  • Give effectively. Organizations like the National Alliance to End Homelessness, Feeding America, and GiveDirectly turn donations into measurable outcomes.
  • Give time. Shelters and food banks run on volunteers. One shift a month is more useful than you think.
  • See people. Eye contact, a greeting, a name. It costs nothing and it's where dignity starts.
